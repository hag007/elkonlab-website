Background#

What APA is#

Most human genes have several polyadenylation sites. Alternative cleavage and polyadenylation (APA) produces mRNA isoforms with different 3′ ends, most often with longer or shorter 3′UTRs (Elkon et al. 2013; Tian & Manley 2017). Since the 3′UTR carries binding sites for microRNAs and RNA-binding proteins, APA changes mRNA stability, translation and localization.

APA in proliferation and cancer#

Proliferating cells express mRNAs with shorter 3′UTRs (Sandberg et al. 2008), and cancer cells use shortening to escape microRNA repression of oncogenes (Mayr & Bartel 2009). Proliferation is associated with global 3′UTR shortening and with more cleavage at intronic poly(A) sites; genes encoding 3′-end processing proteins are more highly expressed in proliferating cells, and E2F transcription factors contribute to this (Elkon et al. 2012). Loss of the CFIm25 subunit (NUDT21) shortens 3′UTRs and increases tumour growth in glioblastoma (Masamha et al. 2014). APA changes are also found during colorectal cancer development (Morris et al. 2012).

Regulators#

The core machinery includes CPSF, CstF, CFIm and CFIIm. Other proteins tune site choice; for example, PABPN1 suppresses the use of proximal sites (Jenal et al. 2012). APA can also produce downstream uncapped RNA isoforms (Malka et al. 2022).

Mapping APA#

3′-end sequencing methods, such as PAS-seq and QuantSeq, measure polyA site usage directly. DaPars and QAPA infer APA from standard RNA-seq.

3′UTR regulation and genetics#

MicroRNA targets are predicted by tools such as TargetScan. RNA-binding proteins can control microRNA access, for example Pumilio on p27 (Kedde et al. 2010). Genetic variants can disrupt polyadenylation signals and change 3′UTR usage (Shulman & Elkon 2020). 3′aQTL mapping shows that APA variation explains part of GWAS heritability (Li et al. 2021).

Open questions#

  • APA in single cells and in specific cell states.

  • Which APA changes are causal and which are by-products of transcription changes.
